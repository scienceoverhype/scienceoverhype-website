Should you take a vitamin D supplement?

By Nitish Vaibhav · Last reviewed 19 Nov 2026 · Educational, not medical advice

The short answer

Partly. Vitamin D supplements make sense for a real deficiency, and low levels are common in India, where a review found about two-thirds of adults below 20 ng/mL. But in VITAL, a 5-year trial of 25,871 adults, 2,000 IU a day didn't meaningfully cut cancer or heart disease in mostly healthy people. Test if your doctor advises, and never megadose.

Why are so many Indians low in vitamin D?

India gets plenty of sun, yet a systematic review of South Asian studies found about 67% of Indian adults below 20 ng/mL, a common cut-off for deficiency. Siddiqee et al., BMC Public Health, 2021 Likely reasons:

  • Indoor work and avoiding midday sun.
  • Darker skin (more melanin) needs more sun to make the same amount.
  • Air pollution blocks some of the UVB light the skin needs.
  • Few foods naturally contain much vitamin D.

How many people count as deficient depends heavily on which cut-off is used, so headlines like "everyone is deficient" overstate it.

What does vitamin D actually do?

Its clearest job is helping the gut absorb calcium for healthy bones. A true deficiency can weaken bones (rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults). Claims that extra vitamin D prevents most diseases have not held up in large trials.

Does taking vitamin D prevent cancer or heart disease?

The largest trial, VITAL, gave 25,871 adults (men 50+, women 55+) either 2,000 IU of vitamin D a day or a placebo, for a median of 5.3 years. Vitamin D did not meaningfully lower the rate of invasive cancer or major heart and stroke events. Most participants were not deficient at the start, so the result applies to mostly healthy people, not to fixing a deficiency. Manson et al., New England Journal of Medicine, 2019

The Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline also advises against routine vitamin D testing for healthy adults under 75, while suggesting supplements for specific groups such as older adults and pregnant women. Indian guidance and your doctor's judgement may differ, especially where deficiency is common. Demay et al., Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism, 2024

Who should consider a supplement?

  • People with a deficiency confirmed by the 25-hydroxy vitamin D blood test.
  • People with little sun exposure, darker skin, older age or conditions that affect absorption, if their doctor advises it.
  • Pregnant women and older adults, under medical guidance.

Can you take too much vitamin D?

Yes. Vitamin D is stored in the body, so very high doses build up. Excess raises blood calcium, which can cause nausea, confusion and kidney damage; case reports from India describe this after repeated high-dose injections. Take the dose your doctor prescribes, and don't self-prescribe large weekly doses or injections.

Frequently asked questions

Should everyone in India take vitamin D?

No. Deficiency is common, but the right step is to test if your doctor advises it and treat a real deficiency, not to supplement blindly.

What did the VITAL trial find?

In 25,871 adults followed for about 5 years, 2,000 IU of vitamin D a day did not meaningfully reduce invasive cancer or major cardiovascular events compared with placebo.

What vitamin D level counts as deficient?

Below 20 ng/mL is a commonly used cut-off for deficiency, though guidelines differ.

Can vitamin D be toxic?

Yes, at very high doses over time. It raises blood calcium and can damage the kidneys. Follow your doctor's dose.
